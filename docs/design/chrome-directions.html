Three axes below, all independent: palette (which green), mode (day or night — this is the real light/dark pair, separate from your browser theme), and treatment (flat panels versus ink-and-grain materials). The rules

What holds regardless of which green

One focal accent

"Exactly one focal accent per map (gold mosaic, glowing treasure pile, altar, hearth)."

One green thing per screen — the action the DM came for. Three accents mean none.

Wrong — three focals
Right — one focal, two quiet

Tint by day, glow by night

"Lighting is painted, not neutral — every light source gets a soft radial warm glow."

Glow only works on a dark ground; on paper it looks like a printing error. So the hover behaviour genuinely differs per mode — a flat tint by day, light rising from below at night. Switch modes on the stage above and hover a row to feel it.

Ink weight hierarchy

"Line weight is heavier on structure, lighter on ground clutter."

Panel frames take the heavy ink; dividers take the hairline. Today both apps share one --border, which is exactly why panels read flat. This is what the treatment switch changes.

Structure — panel frame
Control — buttons, inputs
Clutter — list dividers

Semantic is not accent

"State encodings never rely on colour alone." — PRODUCT.md

Green chrome creates a real collision: green already means connected. The fix is shape, not hue — a filled disc is here, a hollow ring is away, a triangle is trouble. It reads on a bad panel, in a dim room, colour-blind or not.

Where the goblin appears

Thresholds only — never during play

Visible between scenes, invisible during them. Four moments, nowhere else. Placeholder silhouette — the painted version goes through the art style guide like any other asset.

Empty editor Nothing drawn yet. The goblin waits, quill ready. The one place a mascot earns its keep.
Loading a scene Lighting the torches… Replaces a spinner. The torch is the one warm thing in the chrome — it belongs to the map's world, not the UI's.
Player join page You've got a seat at Sai's table. The only moment players meet the goblin — and the highest-traffic screen in the product.
Destructive confirm Delete the Sunken Vestry? It took four hours to draw. The nervousness survives here as a wink — never as the texture of the relationship.

Not negotiable: nothing whimsical goes near a trust claim. "Players only receive what you've revealed" stays literal, in plain language, with no goblin anywhere near it. Charm sells the product; it does not vouch for the security model.

What lands in code

Both apps already declare these names — session/client/src/index.css on :root, canvas/src/index.css on .dark. Going light-first means the editor's currently-unused light block finally does work, and the runner needs a light block it has never had: today it hard-codes one dark theme on :root with a comment explaining that the table has only one theme. What I need from you

DecisionWhere it stands
Which green Moss reads calmest and sits nearest the map art. Verdigris is the most distinctive and the most obviously "designed". Goblin is the brand colour but is a lot to look at for a four-hour session.
Which mode is the default at the table Day suits the editor. For live play, PRODUCT.md's own reasoning still holds — a DM in a dim room with the map carrying every bright pixel. My recommendation: editor defaults to day, table defaults to night, both switchable, and the choice remembered per user rather than per app.
Flat or ink-and-grain Flat is nearly free. Ink-and-grain is what makes chrome look made by the same hand as the maps, and costs a shared stylesheet. It survives both modes, which the vellum idea from last round did not.
Serif panel headers, or only in marketing Most of what makes the ink treatment feel authored. It also slows scanning slightly. Headers only, or nothing in-app.
Does the goblin get a "quiet" toggle Costs nothing, buys goodwill from DMs who want zero personality mid-session. Recommendation: yes, and it hides thresholds only, never functionality.
